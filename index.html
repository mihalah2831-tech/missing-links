Econ notes

HOW IS THIS ECONOMICS?

Every part of the game is an economics problem about knowing what causes what.

The whole game

Causal density

Obesity has many causes working at once: what we eat, how tasty and cheap food is, how much we move, where we live. They interact and shift together, so just watching can't separate them. Economists face the same problem with inflation, growth and inequality.

Your hunch + tinted lens

Confirmation bias

Your brain forms a hunch from the first clues it catches. Once you back a theory, evidence that fits feels bigger and evidence against it is easy to miss. Careful researchers state their hypothesis in advance and go looking for ways it could be wrong.

Unconfirmed points

The illusion of knowing

Anecdotes and flashy one-off results make it feel like you know a lot, and the most exciting numbers are often the least reliable. When they're re-tested, many vanish. Your peak score is how sure you felt. Your real score is what survived.

"It's all ___!"

Simple isn't the same as true

One neat cause is appealing, but a simpler story only wins if the evidence backs it. A single solid counterexample can break a one-cause theory.

Think rounds

Real studies, real traps

Every think round is a real obesity study or policy: soda taxes, menu labels, diet trials, exercise studies, supermarket openings, even fat lab animals. Each camp blames one cause (sugar, calories, diet, sitting, the environment), and each has evidence with holes: hidden third factors, self-selection, backwards arrows, measuring the wrong thing, or too many changes at once. Economists fight back with lotteries, natural experiments and careful comparison groups.

Soda tax level

The seen and the unseen

A policy's first effect is easy to see: soda sales fall. The rest of the chain is harder to spot. People may switch to juice or shop across the border, and the cost can weigh more on tight budgets. Good policy analysis follows the whole chain.

Rule & reward questions

Incentives and unintended consequences

People aren't passive. When a rule or reward changes, they adapt, sometimes in ways nobody planned. So a policy can change the very system you were trying to measure.

MEGA STUDY + Clear Eyes

Closing the knowledge gap

There's always a gap between what we think we know and what's true. You can't see clearly for free: Clear Eyes only charges up from solid evidence. Opening them doesn't make the rumours go away, but it reveals hidden causes that were falling all along, like sleep, stress, genes and money. A MEGA STUDY floods in real clues while rumours dry up. Bigger, better research and honest self-checking shrink the gap, but only while the effort continues. That's why both wear off.
